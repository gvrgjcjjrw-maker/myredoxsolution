Thirty Years a Skeptic — My Redox Solution
My Redox Solution
A confession from a thirty-year skeptic

For thirty years I refused to put my name on a company like this.

Then something found me that I wasn't looking for — an unexpected invitation to give a doctor five minutes. I spent my career in corporate finance, finding the flaw before it cost anyone money. So I did to it what I'd do to any deal: I tried to tear it apart. I couldn't. This is the first time in three decades I've said yes — and here's exactly why.

Why I finally said yes ↓

Decades ago I dabbled — lightly — in the most famous name in this whole industry. I went to the functions. I liked the people. But I never once planted my flag. Something always felt off, and over the years I watched the promises quietly fail to hold up in the lives of the very people who'd made them to me.

So I stayed a skeptic. And when ASEA finally showed up, it didn't arrive as a pitch I went hunting for — just a chance invitation to sit and hear a doctor for five minutes. Right place, right time; I still can't fully explain why it landed in my lap and no one else's. It's been almost ten years, and in all that time not one other person has ever brought it to my attention. It came once — and I nearly waved it off. Instead, I did what I always do: I went looking for the reason to say no — the catch that would let me close the tab and stay comfortable being right about all of it.

Please, let the leadership be crooks.

What I found was a man who turned down a fortune from a pharmaceutical buyer — because the deal meant shelving the technology instead of selling it. Damn.

Please, let the person who brought it to me have an angle.

He was my own financial advisor — already managing my money, with nothing to gain by handing me one more thing. No angle.

Please, let it be just another me-too bottle.

For the first time, I couldn't figure out how anyone would copy it — years of work wrapped in a wall of patents. Not a label you slap on a cheaper bottle.

And the honest one.

Is this network marketing? Yes. It is. I'm not going to hide that or dress it up — you'd smell it on me if I did. I went in eyes wide open about exactly what it is. But that was the wrong question for me. I wasn't deciding whether to build a business. I was testing a product — and whether it was real.

My last, best out: "It's just expensive salt water."

Honestly my favorite, because on its face it's true — it IS a salt solution, made in Salt Lake City of all places. You couldn't write a better punchline.

But here's where I got stuck. Back in 2012, a team of PhDs at the North Carolina Research Campus ran a published, double-blind study on it. Their expectations were, to put it kindly, modest. Their own data surprised them.

I could tell you what they found. But you'd be right not to trust a guy with a link at the bottom of the page. So don't take my word for any of it — take theirs.

Don't listen to me. Listen to the scientist.

By the time I'd worked through leadership, the people, the "commodity" question, and finally the science, something strange was happening. I was disappointed I couldn't kill it. Because a skeptic who can't find the catch doesn't relax. He gets a little annoyed — and then, if he's honest, he starts to dig.

So here's the only instruction I'll ever give you.

Don't share it. Don't sell it. Don't recommend it to a soul. Prove it on your own body first — ninety days, and spend the whole time trying to catch it lying to you. If it can't earn your name, it doesn't get your name.

That reluctance you feel right now — the flinch at putting your name on another one of these — isn't a weakness to coach out of you. It's the most trustworthy thing about you. You just had it aimed at the wrong things. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to do what I did: go looking for the catch. I couldn't find it. I don't think you will either.
